1. The whole project, in short

Say this (the short answer, about 90 seconds; anyone can give it)

Our project is about detecting AI-generated images: pictures made by AI tools such as Stable Diffusion or Midjourney instead of a camera. These pictures now look so real that they can be used for fake news, fake evidence and fraud, and there are far too many images for people to check by eye. So we need computer programs, called detectors, that can spot them.

This semester our job was a literature review. We did not build a detector for this report; we studied what other researchers have built. We fixed our search rules first, found 101 records, removed 26 duplicates, screened 75 and kept 74. The 48 main studies among them were read in full and compared in one big table.

3. What we found

The five families of detectors (Chapter 9)

We sorted detectors by where their clue comes from, because that decides how well a detector copes with a new generator.

FamilyWhat clue it usesThink of it asStudiesMain weakness

The comparisons in the "Think of it as" column are our own, to help explain; they are not in the report. Three studies fit two families, so the families hold 44 different studies; four dataset studies are discussed separately: 44 + 4 = 48.

Our three main findings (Chapter 14)

  1. The problem is built in ("structural"). A detector mostly learns the hidden traces of the one generator it trained on. A new generator that is built differently does not leave those traces, so the detector fails on it. Examples: 99.9% → 54.9% on Midjourney images (Zhu et al., 2023); 92.77% → 65.77% on realistic images from the internet (Yan et al., 2025a).
  2. What helps most. The best results on new generators come from (a) a big general model that was not trained for detection, kept unchanged (such as CLIP), (b) feeding the detector something computed from the image instead of the raw image, or (c) training on many different generators. None of these fully solves the problem.
  3. Testing on new generators and explaining decisions rarely meet. 36 of 48 studies test on new generators, only 4 explain their decisions, and only 1 does both.

The research gap (Section 13.6)

Say this

No study does all four of these together: (1) several well-known pretrained CNNs trained in the same way, (2) a Vision Transformer trained in the same way for comparison, (3) heat maps showing which part of the image the models use, and (4) a test on generators the models never saw. Each piece exists somewhere; the combination does not. Our Semester 8 study is designed to do all four.

Five problems the field still faces (Chapter 13)

  • New generators: detectors trained on one generator fail on others.
  • Compression and editing: sharing an image on social media compresses it, and strong compression can wipe out most of the traces. 10 of the 48 studies admit this weakness.
  • Biased and outdated datasets: for example, GenImage's real and fake images differ in file compression and size, so a model can "cheat"; and every dataset's list of generators goes out of date.
  • Weak explanations: heat maps show where a model looked, not what it found.
  • Hard to compare: studies report different measures on different test sets, so their numbers cannot be ranked fairly.

A4.What were your objectives? Rohit

Careful: do not call it "the first" or "the most complete" review; the report does not claim that.

A6.What is the practical use of your work? Rohit

Anyone choosing or building a fake-image detector can use our master table to see each detector's score next to how it was tested. The review warns that a high score often drops on new generators or real-world images, so one headline number should not be trusted. It also gives our Semester 8 experiment a clear, evidence-based design.

Careful: we have not built an app or tool; do not claim the project can detect fakes today.

A7.Can your work detect deepfake videos or fake audio? Rohit

No. We covered still images only; video and audio were left out on purpose. This report also builds no detector, because it is a review, and the Semester 8 study works on still images too.

B. How we did the review

B1.How did you find the papers? Vishal

Before collecting anything, in Week 1, we wrote a search protocol: where to search, 8 fixed search phrases (such as "synthetic image detection", "CIFAKE" and "cross-generator generalization") and the rules for keeping or dropping a paper. In Week 2 we ran the phrases on arXiv, on publisher and conference websites (IEEE, ACM, CVF, NeurIPS and others) and on Google Scholar. The search gave 101 records.

Careful: our protocol named six databases, but do not say "we searched six databases". Say what Section 8.1 says: arXiv, publisher and conference pages, and Google Scholar. If asked how many records came from each source: we kept the hits by topic (six strands of 16 to 18 records each, Section 8.1), not by database, so do not invent per-database numbers.

B2.How did you decide which papers to keep? Vishal

A paper had to be about still images, be published from 2019 to 2026, report numbers (such as accuracy), and be peer-reviewed or a well-cited preprint. Video-only, audio-only, no-number, non-English and unopenable papers were dropped. From 101 records we removed 26 duplicates, screened the 75 left by title and abstract, and kept 74: 48 main studies, 11 supporting studies and 15 background papers. Figure 8.1 (page 28) shows this flow in the PRISMA style, a standard way of drawing how a review picked its papers.

B3.Only one of 75 records was dropped. Isn't that too few? Vishal

Clearly off-topic results, such as video-only papers, were dropped while searching, so they were never counted in the 101. Only one paper failed our rules: TruthLens, a preprint with a single citation. It was dropped in Week 3, when we checked citation counts, because it failed the "well-cited preprint" rule.

Careful: that is why the report calls Figure 8.1 "PRISMA-style", not a full PRISMA diagram. If asked how many were dropped while searching: we did not count them, so do not guess a number.

If asked what "well cited" means: our protocol set no fixed number of citations, which is a weakness. In practice the gap was large: TruthLens had 1 citation, while two supporting preprints we kept had 77 and 34 when we checked in Week 3. Only 1 of the 48 main studies is a preprint at all (Durall et al., 2019).

B4.Why 2019 to 2026? And why does your abstract say 2019 to 2025? Vishal

2019 to 2026 is the rule for what could be included; we set it in Week 1 to keep the project to a size we could finish. The 48 main studies we found happen to run from 2019 to 2025. The date rule applies only to detection studies, so older background papers, such as the 2014 paper that introduced GANs, are allowed.

B5.Did you read all 48 papers in full? How did you record them? Vishal

The 48 main studies were read from their full texts in Week 3. Each became one row of a table with 8 fixed facts: the study, its method, its model, its dataset, its generators, its reported result, whether it was tested on new generators, and the weakness its authors admit. When a value could not be confirmed we marked it "to verify" instead of guessing; 10 cells were marked, and all 10 were later settled from the papers.

Careful: say only what is true for you about the reading. Be ready to explain three or four papers: Wang et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2023 (GenImage); Bird and Lotfi, 2024 (CIFAKE); Ojha et al., 2023 (CLIP).

B6.How did you make sure your references are real and correct? Vishal

A paper could be cited only if it was on our screening list and in our reference file under the same code (such as C07). C. How fake images are made

C1.What is an AI-generated image? Vishal

A picture made by a computer model, called a generator (for example a GAN or a diffusion model), instead of being taken by a camera. Detection here is a yes-or-no decision: the detector looks at one image and labels it real or AI-generated.

C2.What is a GAN? Vishal

GAN stands for generative adversarial network. Two networks compete: the generator turns random numbers into an image, and the discriminator tries to tell real images from generated ones. The generator learns only from the discriminator's verdict. It builds the image by enlarging a small grid step by step ("upsampling"), and this step is the most studied source of the traces that detectors look for.

C3.What is a diffusion model, and how is it different from a GAN? Vishal

There is no competition. During training, noise is added to real images step by step until only noise remains, and the model learns to remove that noise one step at a time. To make a new image it starts from pure noise and keeps removing noise until a picture appears. Stable Diffusion is a "latent" diffusion model: guided by a text prompt, it does this on a small compressed version of the image instead of on full-size pixels, which made it cheap enough for ordinary computers. A fixed decoder then turns the result into the full-size picture, and that decoder leaves its own trace, which some detectors use (Section 7.3).

C4.What traces do generators leave behind? Vishal

Mostly tiny repeating patterns in the fine detail of the image. They show up when the image is turned into frequencies (its "spectrum"); the eye cannot see them. Diffusion images have fewer such traces than GAN images, but not none: a study of fifteen generators found traces in every family (Corvi et al., 2023a). The traces are fragile, because compression and resizing remove much of them.

Careful: say "fewer traces" for diffusion images, never "no traces".

C5.Why does compression hurt detection so much? Vishal

The traces live in the finest detail, and JPEG compression throws exactly that detail away. At 85% JPEG quality, the frequency patterns of fakes from most of the generators tested became almost impossible to tell apart from real ones (Dzanic et al., 2020). Every social media platform compresses images by default, so this is an everyday problem, not an attack. 10 of the 48 studies name weak results on compressed, resized, blurred or low-resolution images as their own weakness.

C6.Is an AI-generated image the same as a deepfake? Why do some face-editing studies appear in your table? Vishal

Not exactly. In everyday use (general knowledge, not from our report), a deepfake usually means a real person's face or voice in a video or photo that AI has swapped or changed, while an AI-generated image is made entirely by a generator, with no camera photo behind it. Our rule dropped deepfake work on video only or audio only (Table 8.1), so a few still-image studies of face edits stayed in. Only one main study covers nothing but face-manipulation methods (Tsigos et al., 2024, on FaceForensics++), and Table 12.2 counts it separately.

D. Detection methods

D1.What are your five families, and why group them this way? Hardik

We grouped detectors by where their clue comes from: (1) hand-made and frequency checks, 14 studies; (2) CNNs with transfer learning, 19; (3) transformers, 2; (4) detectors that reuse a big frozen model such as CLIP, 8; (5) explanation work such as Grad-CAM, 4. We chose this grouping because how well a detector handles a new generator depends on how closely its clue is tied to the generator it trained on.

If asked why the counts add to 47: three studies are counted twice, because they fit two families, so the families hold 44 different studies. The other four are dataset studies that sit outside the families: 44 + 4 = 48.

D2.What is the hand-made (frequency) family, and why does it fail? Hardik

Here a person decides what to measure, for example the image's frequency pattern, and a simple classifier decides real or fake. Early results were near-perfect, such as 100% on one face dataset. But the clue comes from one design choice of the generator: changing a single upsampling layer cut detection from 84.8–99.8% to 0–0.3% (Chandrasegaran et al., 2021). Compression also erases these clues.

D3.What is a CNN, and what is transfer learning? Hardik

A CNN (convolutional neural network) looks at an image through small filters stacked in layers and learns its own clues during training. Transfer learning means starting from a network that was already trained on ImageNet, a huge collection of everyday photos, and then adapting it to the real-or-fake task. The network already knows general shapes and textures, so it does not start from zero.

D4.What is the difference between feature extraction and fine-tuning? Hardik

In feature extraction the pretrained network is frozen and only a small new classifier on top is trained. In fine-tuning all the network's weights are updated, so the whole network adapts. Feature extraction is cheaper; fine-tuning can adapt more but needs more computing. Comparing both ways for every model is part of our Semester 8 plan.

Careful: the report does not say which way is better. Do not quote Semester 8 numbers as a finding of this report.

D5.What is a Vision Transformer, and how is it different from a CNN? Hardik

A Vision Transformer (ViT) cuts the image into small square patches and uses "self-attention", so every patch can use information from every other patch from the very first layer. A CNN instead widens its view slowly through stacked small filters. The ViT's first step turns each 16×16-pixel block into one vector in a single step. Section 9.3 of our report says this first step discards part of the fine detail that detectors try to keep; in return the ViT sees links across the whole image.

If pushed: a 16×16 colour block is 768 numbers, and ViT-B/16 turns it into a vector of 768 numbers, so in principle nothing has to be thrown away. The real point is that the detail inside each block is mixed in one step instead of being scanned by small filters, so fine traces are easier to miss. You can say that "discards" in our report is a simplification of this.

D6.Why is the ViT only a benchmark and not your proposed model? Hardik

Only 2 of our 48 studies are transformer-based, and in most papers switching to a transformer also changes the training data and the training method at the same time, so we cannot tell what caused the improvement. In Semester 8 we therefore train the ViT with the same data, split, seed, image size and stopping rule as the CNNs, and use it as a reference point, not as a claimed improvement.

Careful: do not say "exactly the same conditions". In our runs three things still differed: the ViT's ready-made weights were most likely pretrained on a bigger dataset (ImageNet-21k, then ImageNet-1k; the CNNs used ImageNet-1k only), it used a different optimiser (AdamW instead of SGD), and a smaller batch (64, like VGG19). So if asked why the ViT scored best, say these differences mean we cannot give the credit to attention alone.

D7.What is CLIP, and how is it used to detect fakes? Hardik

CLIP is a large model trained on millions of pictures with their captions; it was never trained to detect fakes, yet real and fake images turn out to be separable in its features. Ojha et al., 2023 kept CLIP frozen and trained only a very simple classifier on top, and on new generators this beat earlier detectors by 25.90% in accuracy. Weaknesses of this approach: Ojha et al. admit nobody knows exactly why it works, and later CLIP-based detectors were found to get weaker under blur, cropping and compression (Koutlis and Papadopoulos, 2024).

Careful: 25.90% is an improvement over earlier detectors, not an accuracy score.

D8.What is Grad-CAM? Hardik

Grad-CAM draws a heat map over the image showing which regions pushed the model towards its decision. It works in three steps. First, it takes one layer of the network (usually the last convolutional layer), which holds many "feature maps"; each map shows where in the image one kind of pattern was found. Second, it gives each map one weight: the average of its gradient, which says how strongly the decision score reacts to that map. Third, it adds up the weighted maps, keeps only the positive values, and enlarges the result to the size of the image, which gives a rough heat map.

D9.Why does explainability matter, and what is its limit? Hardik

A detector that only says "fake, 97%" gives no reason, which is a problem when its decision is used as evidence or to judge a news photo. But a heat map shows where the model looked, not what it found there, and forensic traces are often invisible anyway. Only 4 of the 48 studies analyse explanations, and only one of them (Chai et al., 2020) also tests on new generators.

D10.Why do you plan Grad-CAM, when the one study that compared explanation methods rated LIME best? Hardik

That comparison (Tsigos et al., 2024) is narrow: one face-manipulation detector on one dataset, FaceForensics++ (Sections 9.5 and 13.4), so it does not show that LIME is better for every detector. Grad-CAM is the standard tool (Section 9.5), and the CIFAKE authors used it to expose a shortcut: their model relied on small flaws in the image backgrounds. Checking our own models for shortcuts like that, on right and on wrong answers, is exactly what our Semester 8 plan needs (Table 14.1).

Careful: do not call Grad-CAM the best explanation method; the report does not say that. Grad-CAM was made for the feature maps of a convolutional layer (Section 9.5), so it suits the Vision Transformer less well; a better method for the ViT is still to be agreed with our guide (Section 5 of these notes).

D11.What is the difference between machine learning and deep learning? Where does your review show it? Hardik

In classic machine learning a person decides which features to measure, and a simple model such as an SVM makes the decision from them. In deep learning a neural network with many layers learns its own features straight from the images. Our families show both: in the hand-made family a person chooses the clue, for example the image's frequency pattern fed to an SVM or k-means (Section 9.1), while in the CNN family the network learns the clue during training (Section 9.2). The two definitions are general knowledge; the examples are from our report.

E. Datasets and metrics

E1.Which datasets did you compare? Vishal

Table 10.1 compares 11 datasets. The main ones: ForenSynths, the classic GAN-era set (train on ProGAN, test on 11 sets); GenImage, a large benchmark with 8 generators; CIFAKE, tiny 32×32 images from one generator; and Chameleon, realistic images collected from the internet. ArtiFact (25 methods) and Community Forensics (4,803 generators) stand out for covering many generators.

E2.What is CIFAKE? Why is it criticised, and why will you still use it? Vishal

CIFAKE (Bird and Lotfi, 2024) has 60,000 real photos from CIFAR-10 and 60,000 fakes from Stable Diffusion v1.4, all tiny 32×32 images. The authors' small CNN reached 92.98%, but Grad-CAM showed it relied on small flaws in the image backgrounds, which belong to that one generator, so accuracy on CIFAKE says little about other generators. We still use it because it is small and balanced, which makes a fair comparison of five models possible on modest hardware, and we add a GenImage test with new generators to cover its weakness.

Careful: 92.98% is Bird and Lotfi's published result, not ours.

E3.What is a dataset "shortcut" or bias? Vishal

A difference between the real and fake images that is not a true trace of AI generation, so a model can score high without really detecting anything. Grommelt et al., 2024 found that GenImage's real and fake images differ in JPEG compression and image size. After removing that bias, accuracy on new generators rose by more than 11 points (for example, a ResNet50 from 71.68% to 82.74%).

E4.Explain the confusion matrix. Vishal

We call the AI-generated image the "positive" class. A fake called fake is a true positive (TP); a fake passed as real is a false negative (FN); a real image called real is a true negative (TN); a real image flagged as fake is a false positive (FP). All the metrics in Chapter 11 are built from these four counts (Figure 11.1, page 49).

E5.Define accuracy, precision, recall and F1. Why is accuracy alone not enough? Vishal

Accuracy is the share of all answers that are right. Precision: of the images flagged as fake, how many really are fake (false alarms lower it). Recall: of all the fakes, how many were caught (misses lower it). F1 combines precision and recall and is high only when both are high. Accuracy is fair only when the test set has equal numbers of real and fake images, and all four depend on the chosen cut-off (threshold) for calling an image fake.

Easy example: a smoke alarm. Precision asks how many alarms were real fires; recall asks how many real fires it noticed.

If asked to write the formulas (Chapter 11): Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN); Precision = TP / (TP + FP); Recall = TP / (TP + FN); F1 = 2 × Precision × Recall / (Precision + Recall). For the ROC curve, the false-alarm rate is FPR = FP / (FP + TN).

E6.What are ROC, AUC, AP and mAP? Vishal

If we try every possible cut-off and plot "fakes caught" against "real images wrongly flagged", we get the ROC (receiver operating characteristic) curve; AUC is the area under that curve, where 0.5 means guessing and 1.0 means perfect. AP (average precision) does a similar job for the curve of precision against recall, and mAP (mean average precision) is the average of the AP values over several test sets, usually one per generator. AUC and AP do not depend on one chosen cut-off.

Careful: the 90.8 of Wang et al., 2020 is an mAP figure, not an accuracy percentage.

E7.Which detector is the best? Why don't you rank them? Hardik

We deliberately do not rank detectors across papers. Studies use different measures on different test sets, one measure cannot be converted into another, and about 23 of the 48 report accuracy alone. So the results can sit side by side but cannot be fairly ranked. That is why we argue for testing models under one common setup, which is exactly what our Semester 8 study does.

Careful: do not name a single "best detector" as a finding of the review.

F. Findings and the research gap

F1.What are the main findings of your review? Hardik

First, the generalisation problem is built in: a detector mostly learns the traces of the one generator it trained on, so its accuracy often falls sharply on new ones that are built differently. Second, the best results on new generators tend to use one or more of three ideas: a big general model that was not trained for detection; an input computed from the image instead of the raw image (for example, how each pixel relates to its neighbours); or training data from many generators. Third, testing on new generators and explaining decisions rarely meet: 36 of 48 studies do the first, only 4 do the second, and only one does both.

F2.Explain your two headline drops. Hardik

In the GenImage benchmark (Zhu et al., 2023), a ResNet-50 trained on Stable Diffusion v1.4 images is 99.9% accurate on that same generator but only 54.9% on Midjourney images, even though both are diffusion models. In the Chameleon study (Yan et al., 2025a), the authors' own detector, AIDE, scores 92.77% on a standard benchmark but only 65.77% on realistic images from the internet. High scores on the training generator or on a benchmark do not carry over.

Careful: 54.9% is on Midjourney only. 92.77% and 65.77% belong to one detector (AIDE), not to "all detectors". Both are other researchers' numbers.

F3.You call the problem "structural". What does that mean? Hardik

It means the problem is built into how detectors learn; it is not a bug that more tuning can fix. Much of what a detector learns is a side effect of one generator's design, such as its upsampling layer or its decoder (the part that turns a compressed result back into the full picture). When a new generator changes that design, the evidence disappears instead of just becoming harder to find.

F4.What exactly is the research gap? Hardik

No study does all four together: several pretrained CNNs trained the same way; a Vision Transformer trained the same way as a reference; heat maps showing where in the image the models look; and a test on generators they never saw. Each piece exists somewhere; for example, the CIFAKE study used Grad-CAM but had no new-generator test. The gap is the combination (Section 13.6, pages 72–73).

Careful: do not say "nobody has used Grad-CAM or Vision Transformers for this". They have; the gap is the combination.

F5.If 36 of 48 studies already test on new generators, what is left to do? Hardik

Testing is common, but most tests check only one move, from one training generator to some new ones, and 15 studies start from the same generator, ProGAN. So these tests show how a detector copes with one particular change, not with new generators in general. Averages also hide the worst case, and benchmarks overstate real-world results (92.77% against 65.77%). And only one study both tests new generators and explains its decisions.

F6.A paper reports 99% accuracy. Isn't the problem solved? Hardik

No. The ResNet-50 that scored 99.9% on its own generator got 54.9% on Midjourney. A high average can hide a total failure on one generator, and in real use the worst case matters more than the average. The Chameleon authors themselves say the problem is far from solved.

F7.Can these detectors be fooled? Hardik

Yes, and several authors say so themselves: small changes made on purpose can fool some classifiers (Frank et al., 2020). Simple things work too: changing one upsampling layer dropped the detection rate of spectrum-based detectors to 0–0.3% (Chandrasegaran et al., 2021), and strong JPEG compression erases frequency traces. Our review records these as the authors' own limitations; we did not test any attack.

F8.What happens when a new generator comes out? Hardik

If it changes the part of the design a detector depends on, the detector's evidence can disappear; this is what happened when GAN-trained detectors met diffusion images. Datasets also go out of date, because each one freezes its list of generators when it is published. Approaches that use general features, computed inputs or very varied training data cope best, but none fully closes the gap.

F9.What is the difference between a cross-dataset test and a cross-generator test? Hardik

A cross-generator test keeps at least one generator out of training and tests on it. A cross-dataset test may only change the photo content while the fakes still come from the same generator. This is exactly why we marked Aghasanli et al., 2023 as "No": their test switched content (ImageNet to pet photos), but all the fakes came from the same Stable Diffusion process.

F10.Why not simply use watermarks instead of detection? Hardik

Our review did not cover watermarking, so this is a general view, not a finding of the report. A watermark helps only if the generator adds one and it survives later editing, and openly released models can be run without adding one. So detectors that examine the image itself are still needed; the two approaches support each other.

F11.In Table 12.1, is the "Key limitation" column your opinion or the authors'? Hardik

Mostly the authors' own: it is the weakness each study admits about itself (Table 8.2). Where a study names no weakness, the cell says so and may add a short note of ours on its scope, for example "None stated by the authors; only GAN generators are evaluated". The note under Table 12.1 says this. We kept the column so that every headline score is read next to its weak point.

Careful: do not say every cell is in the authors' own words; a few cells are our notes on scope.

F12.Your gap is only a combination of things others have done. Is that really a research gap? Hardik

Yes, because only the combination can answer our two key questions together: does a detector still work on generators it never saw, and can we see why it decided? Numbers from separate papers seldom measure the same thing (Section 13.5), so this needs one study that trains several CNNs and a ViT under one common setup, tests them on the same unseen generators and explains them with the same heat-map method. The nearest studies stop short: GenImage puts a ResNet-50 beside transformers, and Coccomini et al., 2022 compare one CNN with one ViT, but neither compares several pretrained CNNs or looks at explanations (Section 12.6).

Careful: present Semester 8 as a careful, fair comparison, not as a new detector or a breakthrough.

9. Simple glossary

AI-generated image: a picture made by a computer model instead of a camera.

Generator: the AI model that makes images, for example Stable Diffusion, Midjourney or ProGAN.

GAN: two networks compete; one makes images and the other tries to catch the fakes.

Diffusion model: makes an image by starting from noise and removing it step by step.

Stable Diffusion: a popular diffusion model guided by a text prompt. CIFAKE's fakes come from version 1.4.

Midjourney: a commercial image generator, used in the 99.9% → 54.9% example.

ProGAN: an older GAN; 15 of our 48 studies train on its images.

Trace (artefact): a tiny hidden pattern that a generator leaves in an image.

Upsampling: enlarging a small grid into a bigger image step by step; the most studied source of traces.

Frequency (spectrum): the image seen as smooth parts and fine detail; traces sit in the fine detail.

JPEG compression: making an image file smaller by throwing away fine detail; strong compression can erase traces.

Detector: a program that labels an image as real or fake.

Binary classification: choosing between two labels, here real or fake.

Deep learning: training neural networks with many layers on many examples.

Weights: the numbers inside a network that hold what it has learned; training adjusts them, unless they are frozen.

Features: numbers that describe an image, such as which edges, textures or patterns it contains. A network learns its own; in the hand-made family a person chooses them.

Classifier: the part that makes the final choice between labels (here real or fake), often a small layer or a simple model placed on top of the features.

CNN: a neural network that looks at an image through small filters arranged in layers.

ImageNet: a large public collection of labelled everyday photos used to pretrain models.

Pretrained model (backbone): a network already trained on ImageNet, reused as a starting point.

Transfer learning: reusing a pretrained network for a new task.

Feature extraction: keep the pretrained network frozen and train only a small new last layer.

Fine-tuning: update all of the network's weights for the new task.

Frozen: weights that are not changed during training.

Vision Transformer (ViT): cuts the image into patches; every patch "pays attention" to every other patch.

Self-attention: a step that lets each part of the input use information from all the other parts.

CLIP: a big model trained on pictures with captions; its features can separate real from fake images.

Grad-CAM: a heat map showing which image regions drove a model's decision.

Explainability: showing why a model made its decision.

Dataset: a collection of labelled images for training and testing.

Benchmark: a standard dataset and test used to compare methods fairly.

CIFAKE: 120,000 tiny (32×32) images: 60,000 real (CIFAR-10) and 60,000 fake (Stable Diffusion v1.4).

GenImage: a large benchmark with 1,331,167 real images and more than a million fakes from 8 generators.

Cross-generator test: testing on a generator the detector never saw in training.

Cross-dataset test: testing on different photos, possibly from the same generator.

In-the-wild images: images collected from the internet as people actually shared them (for example AI-made pictures posted online), instead of images the researchers generated themselves.

Shortcut (bias): an accidental difference, such as file type, that lets a model "cheat".

Confusion matrix: a 2×2 table of right and wrong answers (TP, FP, TN, FN).

Accuracy: the share of all answers that are right.

Precision: of the images flagged as fake, the share that really are fake.

Recall: of all the fakes, the share that were caught.

F1 score: one number combining precision and recall.

Threshold: the score above which an image is called fake.

ROC curve and AUC: fakes caught against false alarms at every threshold; an AUC of 0.5 is guessing and 1.0 is perfect.

AP and mAP: a summary of the precision-recall curve; mAP averages it over several test sets.

Systematic review: a literature review that follows fixed rules written before the search.

PRISMA-style flow: a diagram of how many papers were found, removed and kept.

Primary, secondary and background studies: read in full and tabled; used where needed; classic papers on generators, networks or methods, plus three earlier surveys.

Extraction table: our table of 8 facts recorded for each main study.

Taxonomy: a way of sorting methods into groups (our five families).

Preprint: a paper posted online (for example on arXiv) before peer review.

DOI and arXiv ID: permanent identifiers used to check that a paper is real.

Kaggle: a website with free online notebooks and GPUs; we use it for the Semester 8 training.

GPU: a graphics processor that trains neural networks much faster than a normal processor.

Epoch: one full pass over all the training images.

Seed: the number that starts the random-number generator. Fixing it fixes a run's random choices (data split, shuffling order, flips, the new layer's starting weights), so the run can be repeated; on a GPU an exact repeat also needs the same hardware and software.

Ablation: changing one setting to see how much it matters.
