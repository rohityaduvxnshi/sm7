# 14. CONCLUSION, LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE SCOPE

This chapter brings the report to a close. Section 14.1 sums up what the review found, Section 14.2 sets out the limits within which those findings hold, and Section 14.3 describes the future scope of the work, beginning with the study planned for the Semester 8 major project.

## 14.1 Conclusion

This review examined the deep-learning methods that have been proposed for detecting AI-generated still images. It organised the field into five families according to where a detector's evidence comes from (Chapter 9): handcrafted, statistical and frequency-domain forensics; convolutional networks adapted through transfer learning; transformer-based and attention-based models; detectors that reuse frozen vision-language representations; and work that sets out to explain detection decisions. The review went on to compare the datasets and benchmarks most often used for evaluation (Chapter 10) and the metrics in which results are reported (Chapter 11). Its central comparison placed all 48 primary studies in one master table (Table 12.1). Besides the usual entries, that table carries two further columns, *Cross-generator tested?* and *Key limitation*: the first shows whether a detector was tested on generators missing from its training data, and the second gives the limitation that the study's own authors acknowledge.

The comparison leads to three main findings. First, the generalisation gap is structural and not incidental. A large part of what a detector picks up during training is a by-product of the design of one particular generator. When that design changes, the evidence disappears instead of merely becoming harder to find. The drops reported in the literature are steep: one detector's accuracy falls from 99.9% on its training generator to 54.9% when its test images come from Midjourney instead [C52], and a mean accuracy of 92.77% on a standard benchmark becomes 65.77% on realistic in-the-wild images [C36]. Second, the best cross-generator results are linked by three recurring properties: the representation was not trained on detection data, the detector receives an input computed from the image instead of the image itself, or the training data span many generators. Third, testing on unseen generators and analysing explanations seldom meet in one study. Of the 48 primary studies, 36 include a cross-generator evaluation, but only four include an explanation analysis.

This third finding forms one part of the research gap stated in Section 13.6. The experimental study designed to fill that gap is described in Section 14.3.

## 14.2 Limitations of This Review

The conclusions in Section 14.1 hold only within limits that come from the design of the review, and they should be read with these limits in mind.

The first set of limits concerns coverage. The literature search was performed once, in Week 2 (27 July to 2 August 2026), so any study published after that week lies outside the surveyed set. Under the protocol, only work written in English and available in full text could be included, and detection studies had to date from 2019 to 2026; background references on generators, backbones and explanation methods were exempt from that window. The included studies were also not all read to the same depth. Full-text extraction covered the 48 primary studies, whereas the 11 secondary studies were consulted selectively and have no row in Table 12.1.

The second set concerns the evidence. All performance figures in this report come from the original publications; each was obtained under its own authors' protocol, and none was reproduced for this review. Because the studies combine different metrics with different test suites, their results can be compared directly only with caution (Section 11.2). Lastly, no inter-rater agreement statistic was calculated for screening or for extraction. A statistic of this kind measures how closely independent reviewers agree in their decisions; without one, the consistency of these judgements remains unquantified.

## 14.3 Future Scope

The Semester 8 major project is planned around the combination that Section 13.6 found missing. At its centre is a comparison, based on transfer learning, of four ImageNet-pretrained CNN backbones, namely VGG19, ResNet50, DenseNet121 and EfficientNet-B0 [C72, C73, C74, C75]. Every backbone is to be used in two configurations. In feature extraction, the pretrained layers stay frozen and only a classifier placed on top of them is trained; in fine-tuning, the pretrained weights are updated as well. A Vision Transformer [C70], trained under the same conditions as the CNNs, will act as the benchmark for the comparison.

Each element of this design follows from a finding of the review, and Table 14.1 lists the elements next to the findings behind them. The four backbones make up the family that Section 9.2 identified as the standard choice for transfer learning, although its members are rarely compared with one another on equal terms (Section 12.6). Holding every model to one matched protocol responds to Section 13.5, which argued that architectures should be compared under a common protocol and not through figures gathered from separate papers. The ViT is included as a benchmark and not as a proposed improvement, because Section 9.3 found that when a published study changes its backbone, it usually changes the pretraining data and the training objective at once. Explanations will come from Grad-CAM [C61], applied to incorrect as well as correct predictions, since Section 9.5 showed that an explanation can expose a shortcut while still failing to name the evidence itself. The primary dataset is to be CIFAKE [C51]. Its 120,000 images keep it small enough for a matched comparison of five architectures to remain feasible on modest hardware, but all of its generated images come from a single generator. A subset of GenImage [C52] will therefore supply the cross-generator test, built from generators that the models never saw in training. It is this test that would show whether a result describes one generator or detection more generally.

On the guide's instruction of 15 August 2026, preparation of the code for this study began as a separate work track, which belongs to the Semester 8 major project and will be reported there.

Beyond this study, the challenges set out in Chapter 13 suggest three wider directions for the field. Detectors should be tested routinely on compressed and post-processed images, since the platforms through which suspect images travel compress them by default (Section 13.2). Evaluation should also move past datasets whose generator lists are frozen at publication towards realistic in-the-wild images, on which detectors that do well on standard benchmarks fall sharply (Sections 13.1 and 13.3), and the quality of explanations should be measured quantitatively, which, among the surveyed studies, has been attempted for only one detector on one dataset (Section 13.4). These directions, like the planned study, lie beyond the present review, which contains no experimental result of the team's own.
