# 1. INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Purpose of Plan

This is the final report of the minor project carried out by Group 148 in the seventh semester of the B.Tech programme in Computer Science and Engineering. It was undertaken as the Non-Teaching Credit Course (NTCC) Minor Project, ETMN100, and ran for twelve weeks, from 20 July to 9 October 2026, guided by Dr. Richa Gupta. The report follows Amity's project plan format, so it describes the goals, scope, requirements and management of the project as well as the work itself. That work was a systematic review of deep-learning methods for detecting AI-generated still images, that is, for deciding whether a single image was taken by a camera or produced by a generative model.

The report explains why the review was needed and how it was planned and carried out: a search protocol fixed in the first week, screening of the collected records, full-text extraction of the 48 primary studies into a structured table, and a check of every reference identifier against arXiv, Crossref or proceedings records. It then reports what the comparison of published results showed, especially for generators missing from a detector's training data, and uses the gaps it exposed to shape the experimental study proposed for the Semester 8 major project.

Every experimental result in this report was published by other researchers. No experiment was run for this review, and no model was trained or tested for it; each performance figure quoted is the value reported by its cited authors.

## 1.2 Background Information and Available Alternatives

The first models in wide use for photograph-like images were generative adversarial networks (GANs), introduced in 2014, which train a generator network against a discriminator network [C66]. Later refinements, StyleGAN2 among them, improved GAN output until synthetic human faces could survive close scrutiny [C67]. Diffusion models brought the next shift: instead of pitting two networks against each other, they learn to undo the gradual noising of an image [C68]. Latent diffusion moved this process into a compressed latent space, making text-guided generation cheap enough for ordinary computers and for open release [C69]. A convincing fake photograph therefore no longer needs specialist ability; all it takes is a few seconds and a written prompt. Such pictures already spread across public social media in numbers large enough to be compiled into research datasets [C56].

These capabilities cause real, not hypothetical, problems. A generated image can lend weight to a false claim in the news or serve as fake evidence of an event that never happened. It can also be used to impersonate people, since generated faces are now convincing enough to have their own detection literature [C59]. These threats fall within media forensics, an older field that until recently dealt mainly with edited images rather than fully synthesised ones [C60]. Less visibly, large web-scraped image collections can no longer be taken to hold camera output alone, because synthetic images are posted on the very platforms those collections are drawn from [C56]. For these reasons, recent surveys treat AI-generated media detection as a research problem in its own right [C58].

Checking images by eye cannot keep pace. A large platform receives more uploads in a single day than human reviewers could ever examine. In addition, the cues that detectors exploit are often invisible to a viewer. Many of these cues are statistical traces of the upsampling steps inside the generator, and they show up in the frequency spectrum but not in the picture itself [C06, C08]. For both reasons, the only practical option is automated detection.

A practitioner can choose among five families of automated detection. Handcrafted forensics uses hand-designed statistical or frequency-domain features. CNN-based methods train convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for the task, usually by adapting a network pretrained on a large general image collection, which is called transfer learning. Vision transformers rely on attention instead of convolution. Multimodal detectors reuse features of foundation models such as the vision-language model CLIP. Explainability-oriented work, using tools such as Grad-CAM, shows which image regions drive a decision. Chapter 9 examines each family.

For the project itself, the order of work was set by the Semester 7 scope rule, which makes the minor project a literature review; the experiment planned for the Semester 8 major project is built around the gaps that this review documented.

## 1.3 Statement of the Problem

Automated detection remains an unsolved research problem. A detector with a mean accuracy of 92.77% on a standard benchmark reaches only 65.77% on a benchmark assembled deliberately from realistic in-the-wild images [C36].

The main obstacle is generalisation from one generator to another. A detector tends to perform well on the generator family it was trained on and much worse on other families. Early results looked promising: {C01} reported 90.8 mAP (mean average precision), averaged over eleven test sets, ten of them from generators unseen during training, for a ResNet-50 trained on ProGAN images alone, while cautioning that the result held "for now". Diffusion models largely put an end to this transfer. Detectors trained on GAN images lose, on average, 15.2% AUROC (area under the receiver operating characteristic, or ROC, curve) on diffusion images [C23]. In another evaluation, four GAN-trained detectors tested on twelve generators, five of them diffusion models, averaged roughly 52–76% accuracy on uncompressed images and about 50–61% after the resizing and compression that a social platform applies, with the diffusion models among the hardest cases [C24]. The pattern recurs among diffusion models themselves, where a ResNet-50 that is 99.9% accurate on the generator it learned from reaches only 54.9% on another [C52]. Part of the reason is that many cues used by detectors are tied to one generator's design, not to synthetic images as such. For example, a commonly used spectral cue can be eliminated by modifying the final upsampling layer of the generator, which drops detection from 84.8–99.8% to 0–0.3% [C19].

These results define the problem this review addresses. Published detectors are evaluated under different protocols, and they are rarely tested on unseen generators in the same study that analyses what drives their decisions. A consolidated and checkable picture of which detection approaches generalise is therefore missing. This review sets out to build that picture from the published evidence.

## 1.4 Organisation of the Report

Chapters 2 to 6 cover the planning of the project: goals and deliverables, scope and budget, constraints, requirements, and the timeline, roles and risks.
