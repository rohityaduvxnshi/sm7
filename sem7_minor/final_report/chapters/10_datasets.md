# 10. DATASETS AND BENCHMARKS

Reported accuracy figures can only be read in the light of the data behind them, so this chapter looks at the datasets and benchmarks that the surveyed detectors are trained and tested on. Section 10.1 traces how these datasets developed, from the first GAN benchmarks to in-the-wild collections, and Section 10.2 sets out the problems known to affect them.

## 10.1 From GAN Benchmarks to In-the-Wild Data

The field's evaluation practice was set by ForenSynths, the dataset that {C01} released with their CNN detector. Its lasting value lies in the evaluation design more than in the images. Training uses one generator only: 720,000 images from 20 ProGAN categories, with 18,000 real and 18,000 generated images in each. Performance is then reported separately on eleven test sets: one from ProGAN itself and ten from generators that the detector never sees in training. This design made cross-generator transfer, that is, how well a detector handles generators missing from its training data, a headline figure instead of an afterthought.

Several later detectors are trained on this ProGAN set: some on one, two or four of its twenty categories [C15, C16, C21, C22, C39, C40], one on 200,000 of its images [C43] and one on all twenty categories [C48]. The price of this agreement is that much of the literature trains on a single distribution, so the generalisation results placed side by side all describe one particular step from training data to test data, not the general case. {C37} widened the design on the test side, keeping the ProGAN training set and adding test subsets drawn from diffusion models and from autoregressive models (which build an image one part at a time). The resulting benchmark, UniversalFakeDetect, has nineteen test subsets, now the standard test suite for the CLIP-feature detectors described in Section 9.4 [C37, C41, C48].

Diffusion models called for new data. DiffusionForensics, published with the DIRE detector, sets real images from LSUN-Bedroom and ImageNet against images from eight diffusion models [C25]. GenImage is larger: it pairs 1,331,167 real ImageNet images with over a million generated ones from eight generators (Midjourney, two versions of Stable Diffusion, ADM, GLIDE, Wukong, VQDM and BigGAN). More importantly, it makes cross-generator classification and the classification of degraded images, such as low-resolution, JPEG-compressed or blurred ones, benchmark tasks in their own right [C52]. ArtiFact covers more generators, 25 generation methods across 2,496,738 images, and was designed with robustness as an aim [C53]. CIFAKE sits at the other end of the scale: its 120,000 images pair photographs from CIFAR-10 with Stable Diffusion v1.4 images at a resolution of 32×32 [C51]. Its small size allows training on modest hardware, which is both its attraction and its limitation. D3 is a further diffusion-era collection, released together with a detector trained contrastively [C49].

The third generation of datasets stops relying on a hand-picked list of generators. Community Forensics takes diversity to its limit, collecting 2.7 million images made by 4,803 different generators to test whether a broader range of training data is enough by itself to produce generalisation [C35]. Chameleon heads the other way, gathering realistic images and reporting how accurately detectors classify them [C36]. TWIGMA's images were collected from Twitter, so the dataset reflects what users actually shared, not what a researcher decided to generate [C56]. ## 10.2 Known Problems with the Evaluation Data

Of the weaknesses a dataset can have, the most direct is a shortcut: a property that separates its real images from its generated ones without being a trace of generation. {C31} found two such properties in GenImage. Its real images and its generated ones differ systematically in two respects, JPEG compression and image size, which lets a classifier score highly by reading these properties rather than any forensic trace. Once the bias is removed, in-domain accuracy (accuracy on test images from the training generator) falls, but the result that matters improves: on GenImage's cross-generator task, accuracy went up by more than eleven points, with a ResNet50 improving from 71.68% to 82.74% and a Swin-T from 74.09% to 85.83%. The authors caution that other unwanted biases, such as differences in style and generator fingerprints (Section 7.3), may remain even with these two controlled.

Single-generator datasets show a narrower form of the same weakness. CIFAKE is built from exactly one generator, and its authors' Grad-CAM analysis, which highlights the image regions behind the network's decision (Section 9.5), reports that the trained network relies on small imperfections in the backgrounds of the images [C51]. These imperfections belong to Stable Diffusion v1.4 at a 32×32 resolution, not to synthesis in general, so accuracy on CIFAKE says little about another generator. GenImage measures the gap under matched conditions: a ResNet-50 has an accuracy of 99.9% when trained and tested on images from one generator but only 54.9% on Midjourney images [C52]. A single-generator dataset cannot reveal such a fall, which is why the study planned for Semester 8 (Section 14.3) pairs CIFAKE with a subset of GenImage instead of using CIFAKE alone.

Staleness, meaning that a dataset falls behind the generators released after it, has the same root. Each dataset's generator list is frozen at publication, and the field has responded by lengthening the list: one recent evaluation covers nineteen generators [C34], another eighteen [C38], and Community Forensics 4,803 [C35]. Longer lists keep the benchmarks moving, yet they never quite catch up with whichever generator appeared most recently. Comparability is also harmed when researchers create their own data. Several of the surveyed studies test on sets assembled for their own paper [C08, C14, C17, C64], and {C64} give the reason directly: no open datasets existed for their setting.

The strongest evidence that benchmark scores overstate real-world performance comes from Chameleon. In the study that introduced it, the authors' own detector reaches a mean accuracy of 92.77% on a standard benchmark, AIGCDetectBenchmark, and 86.88% on GenImage, but only 65.77% on the realistic images collected for Chameleon; every detector they tested comes close to failing there, and they judge the problem to be far from solved [C36]. The benchmark figure is thus twenty-seven points above what a detector achieves on images like those it would face in practice. The gap lies in the data, not in any one detector, which is why Chapter 11 turns to the measurement of performance and to how the surveyed studies report it.
