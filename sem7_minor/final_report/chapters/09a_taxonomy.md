# 9. TAXONOMY OF DETECTION APPROACHES

Detectors differ less in the decision they reach than in where the evidence behind it comes from, and this chapter is organised around that point. Five families result. In the first, a person designs the measurement (Section 9.1); in the second, a convolutional network learns it while being trained for detection (Section 9.2). The third trains or fine-tunes a transformer backbone (Section 9.3), the fourth reuses a frozen, general-purpose vision-language representation (Section 9.4), and the fifth contributes an explanation rather than a decision (Section 9.5). Earlier surveys sorted this work by medium or by the type of forgery [C59, C60]. This review prefers sorting by the origin of the evidence because it links the taxonomy to the generalisation problem of Chapter 7: how well a detector handles a new generator depends largely on how closely its evidence is tied to its training generator. The boundaries are not watertight, and the few studies that span two families are noted where they appear. Figure 9.1 summarises the five families and their main techniques.

## 9.1 Handcrafted, Statistical and Frequency-Domain Forensics

In this family a person, not an optimiser, chooses what to measure. A fixed transform, such as a Fourier or discrete cosine transform (DCT), a high-pass filter or a denoising residual, is applied to the image, and its output goes to a simple classifier, in the tradition of classical media forensics [C60]. Chapter 7 explained why most of these transforms are spectral; this section reports what detectors built on them achieved.

Early spectral detectors were accurate and cheap. {C17} averaged the power spectrum of the discrete Fourier transform azimuthally into a one-dimensional profile, classified it with k-means, logistic regression or an SVM, and reported 100% accuracy on their own Faces-HQ set with as few as 20 labelled examples. In a follow-up, {C08} reached 100% with this feature on CelebA but only 85% on individual FaceForensics++ frames, and proposed suppressing the trace by regularising a generator's spectrum. {C07} turned to the DCT domain, in which the grid left by upsampling shows up directly. Applied to DCT coefficients, ridge regression told real and fake FFHQ images apart at 100.00%, compared with 75.78% when the same classifier was given raw pixels, although the artefact is demonstrated on the GANs they tested, not on one withheld from training. {C18} modelled how the spectrum decays at high frequencies and fed the parameters of that fit to a five-nearest-neighbour classifier, which reached 99.2% on uncompressed images of 1024×1024 pixels.

Two findings limit these results. First, the same detector drops to 88.8% on 256×256 images, and after 85% JPEG compression generated and real spectra become essentially indistinguishable [C18]. Second, {C19} retrained generators with their final layer switched to bilinear upsampling, and detection based on spectral decay fell from 84.8–99.8% to 0–0.3%, so the cue stems from a design decision rather than being inherent in CNN-generated images. {C06} had met a form of this earlier: their classifier learned from log spectra of AutoGAN-simulated artefacts, not real generated images, and averaged 97.2% accuracy in leave-one-out testing but failed on GauGAN, whose upsampler differs. Since then, the frequency behaviour of generative models has been studied for its own sake [C20].

Later methods stayed with frequency cues but let the network do more of the work. BiHPF applies bilateral high-pass filters before a ResNet-50 and, after training on four ProGAN classes, reaches a mean accuracy of 84.2% on unseen GANs [C21]. FrePGAN adds a learned frequency-level perturbation in front of that backbone and, also trained on four ProGAN classes, averages 79.4% accuracy across models; its authors openly admit that frequency artefacts are an advantage on the training generator but a hindrance to transfer [C12]. FreqNet applies its convolutions in the fast Fourier transform (FFT) domain, using a network of 1.9M parameters trained with no pretraining, and across 17 GAN test sets it reports a mean accuracy of 92.8%, whereas a baseline built on CLIP features reaches 83.0% [C22, C37]. NPR replaces the spectrum with a local measurement, the relationships between neighbouring pixels that upsampling leaves behind, and reads it with a CNN of 1.44M parameters, obtaining 92.2% mean accuracy on 28 generators spanning GAN, deepfake and diffusion types [C16].

Not all handcrafted features are spectral. {C10} fed co-occurrence matrices of pixel values to a deep CNN and scored above 99% accuracy within each of two GAN datasets; they also trained on one generator family and tested on the other, although only those two families were covered. Another strand targets one specific model rather than a family. {C05} averaged the denoising residuals of images that share a single source, as is done to estimate camera sensor noise, and identified that source with 90.3% accuracy on uncompressed images and 90.1% after JPEG compression at quality 95. {C04} instead learned the fingerprint, reaching 99.43% in a five-way attribution task on CelebA (real images plus four GANs), while a PRNU-style baseline (photo-response non-uniformity) reached 86.61%. Both are closed-set: they choose among known sources rather than flagging a new one. Diffusion-era analysis of this kind exists too, with no detector attached. {C30} compare the artefacts of fifteen GAN, VQ (vector-quantised), diffusion and latent-diffusion models; they give no detection metrics and point out that post-processing may modify the artefacts and hide them. Another contribution offers a dataset and a detector aimed directly at diffusion output [C55].

## 9.2 CNN-Based Approaches and Transfer Learning

Rather than designing the measurement, a study can let a convolutional network learn it, usually through transfer learning: a backbone pretrained on ImageNet, from families such as VGG, ResNet, DenseNet or EfficientNet [C72, C73, C74, C75], provides general features and is fine-tuned on real and generated images. {C01} set the baseline for this family. Their ResNet-50 saw only ProGAN images during training, augmented with blur and JPEG, and averaged 90.8 mAP across eleven test sets, one from ProGAN itself and ten from generators it had never seen, against 90.1 without augmentation. On Photoshop-style manipulations it does no better than chance, and its authors cautioned that the transfer holds "for now".

{C02} narrowed the receptive field so that each decision depends on local patches alone. Their truncated Xception classifier, trained on ProGAN, achieved 99.99 AP on StyleGAN. Because each patch is scored separately, the scores also map where the evidence lies, so the study returns in Section 9.5.

Several studies then pursued universal GAN detection. {C03} compared seven detectors and found that on uncompressed images from unseen GANs several of them exceeded an AUC of 0.9, but compression and resizing caused severe degradation. Their own variant without downsampling went past 90% accuracy, yet the authors judged that reliable tools were still a long way off. {C11} extended that design with contrastive training, achieving an AUC of 0.997 and 95.6% accuracy over seven unseen GAN architectures. Other studies changed where the network looks. Gram-Net relies on global texture statistics; in-domain it scores above 98%, but it manages around 77.7% when trained on StyleGAN and evaluated on DCGAN, DRAGAN and StarGAN [C09]. A ResNet-50 with two branches, fusing global features with patches picked out by attention, achieves a mean AP of 91.73% on the unseen models of a 19-generator set [C14]. An ensemble of five EfficientNet-B4 networks [C74], trained orthogonally, reaches an AUC of 0.9995 on a test set containing StyleGAN3, which was kept out of training [C13].

These figures all came from GANs, and diffusion models erased much of the margin. {C24} tested four GAN-trained detectors on twelve generators, among them the GAN used for training and five diffusion models (ADM, GLIDE, Latent Diffusion, Stable Diffusion and DALL·E 2). Averaged over all twelve, their accuracy was roughly 52–76% on uncompressed images and about 50–61% after compression and resizing, and the diffusion models were among the hardest cases. {C23} quantified this collapse as an average AUROC drop of 15.2% and found it asymmetric: a diffusion-trained detector achieved an average Pd@1%FAR (detection rate at a 1% false-alarm rate) of 94.26% on GANs, but a GAN-trained one achieved 26.34% on diffusion images.

One remedy leaves the classifier unchanged and gives it a map computed from the image instead; NPR, described in Section 9.1, works this way [C16]. LGrad turns an image into the gradients of a pretrained CNN and classifies those. In its single-class setting it averages 86.3% accuracy over eight test models, 11.4 points above FrePGAN, though it swaps reliance on the training data for reliance on the network that computes the gradients [C15]. With diffusion images the map is most often a reconstruction error. DIRE subtracts the DDIM reconstruction of an image from the image itself and passes the residual to a ResNet-50, averaging 99.9% accuracy across eight diffusion models [C25]. SeDID uses the per-step denoising error, and its network variant averages 0.9634 AUC across the three test sets it reports [C27]. LaRE² measures the error in latent space and uses it to refine features, improving accuracy by up to 11.9% over the previous best on eight GenImage generators [C29]. AEROBLADE does away with the classifier and scores an image by how faithfully the latent diffusion model's own autoencoder can rebuild it, giving a mean AP of 0.992 but requiring access to that autoencoder [C28]. FakeInversion inverts Stable Diffusion to obtain a noise map and a reconstruction for each image, and a ResNet-50 trained from scratch takes all three as input, averaging 0.759 AUC-ROC over text-to-image models unseen in training [C33].

The last group alters the training signal and leaves the input alone. DRCT turns real images into hard positives by reconstructing them with a diffusion model, then trains contrastively on these, gaining over 10% accuracy in cross-set tests, though the gain is smaller on GAN images [C32]. ZED uses no generated training images: it fits a lossless coding model to real images and flags those it can encode unusually cheaply, averaging about 90.0% AUC over 19 generators, but is unreliable when inputs are heavily compressed or resized [C34]. Community Forensics attacks the problem through data. Trained on 2.7M images produced by 4,803 generators, it reaches 89.2% accuracy and 0.987 mAP on an out-of-distribution set of 21 models, which in its authors’ view still leaves error rates too high for critical applications [C35].

Overall, the family is moving away from convolutional networks trained from scratch to find forensic features and towards general-purpose pretrained representations, kept frozen in some detectors and fine-tuned in others. DRCT already relies on CLIP-ViT-L/14 as one of its backbones [C32], and Community Forensics classifies with a CLIP-pretrained ViT-S [C35]; such representations are the subject of Sections 9.3 and 9.4.
