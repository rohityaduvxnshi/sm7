# 7. LITERATURE REVIEW

Before a detector can be understood, it has to be clear what evidence it looks for. This chapter supplies that background. It explains how generative adversarial networks (GANs) and diffusion models, the two generator families the literature concentrates on, produce an image, and why their output carries traces that a detector can use. It also positions this review against earlier surveys. The focus is on how generators work; the detection literature itself is analysed in Chapters 9 to 12.

## 7.1 Generative Adversarial Networks

In a GAN, two networks are trained in competition. The generator turns a vector of random numbers into an image, while the discriminator receives both real images and generated ones and has to tell them apart. Training alternates between the two, so the generator is never shown a particular image to reproduce; the only training signal available to it is the discriminator's current verdict on what it produced [C66]. Nothing in this set-up requires the generated images to match real photographs statistically any further than is needed to stop the discriminator objecting, a point taken up in Section 7.3.

In structure, a GAN generator is a sequence of layers that upscales a small feature map stage by stage until full resolution is reached. Later architectures kept this basic form but altered its internals. StyleGAN2, for example, redesigned the normalisation and layer structure of the generator with the specific aim of removing characteristic artefacts seen in the output of the preceding version, and reported higher quality for high-resolution face synthesis [C67]. These changes had two effects. Synthetic faces became hard to recognise as fake by eye, and designing a generator turned partly into a matter of eliminating whichever traces had recently been pointed out.

## 7.2 Diffusion Models

A diffusion model does without the adversarial game and uses a two-part construction instead. In the forward process, a little Gaussian noise is added to a real image at each of many steps, until it can no longer be distinguished from noise. The forward direction is fixed, and nothing in it is learned. The model learns the inverse: shown a noisy image together with its step index, it is trained to estimate the noise that had been added, which allows that step to be undone. To generate, the model starts with pure noise and keeps applying its learned reverse step until a picture emerges [C68]. As every step is only a minor correction rather than one large jump, training escapes the instability associated with adversarial optimisation.

Repeating the denoising step many times on full-resolution images demands heavy computation. Latent diffusion reduces this cost by running diffusion inside the latent space of an autoencoder trained in advance, where images are held in compressed form; diffusion works on a compact latent code, which a decoder finally maps back to pixels. The paper that introduced latent diffusion also brought in cross-attention conditioning, the means by which a text prompt can guide generation [C69]. Many publicly released text-to-image systems rest on this design. For detection, an important feature is the division between a latent diffusion stage and the fixed decoder that follows it, as Section 7.3 explains.

## 7.3 Where Detectable Artefacts Come From

Both generator families build an image through repeated upsampling, and no other source of detectable traces has been studied as much. Convolutional generators that rely on up-convolution fail to match real images in their spectral distribution, and the mismatch is concentrated at high frequencies [C08]. Seen through the discrete cosine transform, the same defect takes the form of regular, grid-like patterns that recur in several GAN architectures [C07]. Direct evidence singles out the upsampling module as the cause: simulating that module alone, with no real generated images involved, can reproduce artefacts typical of a GAN pipeline [C06]. These traces cannot be seen in the image itself; they belong to its frequency spectrum.

An important qualification applies: the traces stem from particular design choices, not from synthesis as such. If a generator's last upsampling layer is replaced by bilinear upsampling, the high-frequency spectral signature on which detectors had come to depend largely disappears [C19]. Likewise, a spectrum-based classifier trained on the artefacts of one upsampler fails to generalise to GauGAN, which is built with a different upsampler [C06]. Once such a trace is known, regularising the generator's spectrum during training can also deliberately suppress it [C08]. Frequency behaviour has accordingly been studied as an attribute of generative models themselves, not merely as a handy cue for detection [C20].

Looked at more closely, the trace identifies a single model rather than a family. Fingerprints learned from generated images are specific enough for an image to be attributed to the exact network that generated it, and models that differ only in their training run, for example in the random seed used at initialisation, are reported to leave fingerprints that can be told apart [C04]. There is also a hand-crafted equivalent: averaging the denoising residuals of many images from a single source, the same procedure that classical forensics uses to estimate a camera's sensor noise, gives a fingerprint capable of identifying the generating model [C05]. In both cases the measurement resembles a serial number more than a general trait of synthetic imagery.

Diffusion models alter this picture without wiping it out. Their output carries fewer detectable traces than GAN output and, under perturbation, lowers detection performance more readily [C23]. It is not trace-free, though. Forensic analysis of diffusion-generated images finds spectral fingerprints broadly of the same kind as those described above [C24], and a comparison of fifteen models spanning GAN, VQ-based (vector-quantised), diffusion and latent-diffusion designs detects artefacts throughout: they differ between families, but none of the families is free of them [C30]. Latent diffusion contributes a further source, specific to its design. Because one fixed decoder is shared by all the images that a given system outputs, that decoder leaves a signature which training-free detectors are able to use [C28]; Section 9.2 covers such detectors. Lastly, every trace described so far is fragile. The traces sit at high frequencies, exactly the information that lossy compression discards; once JPEG compression is strong, a generated image's spectrum becomes essentially indistinguishable from that of a real one [C18]. Ordinary post-processing can also alter or conceal them [C30].

This is why generalisation, introduced in Chapter 1, is a structural difficulty and not an engineering inconvenience. Suppose the evidence that a detector has learned stems from the upsampling operator, the training run or the decoder of one generator. A new generator differing in any one of these then does not just offer harder examples; it takes the evidence away. Much of what distinguishes the approaches reviewed in Chapter 9 is how each tries to get around this dependence, and Chapter 13 comes back to it as the central open challenge facing the field.

## 7.4 Earlier Surveys and the Position of This Review

This review covers published research on the detection of AI-generated still images, within a deliberately narrow scope. It is restricted to single images, so deepfake detection in video and audio is excluded; to work published from 2019 to 2026; and to studies reporting quantitative results, so that the performance claimed in different studies can be set side by side. Chapter 8 gives the full search protocol and the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Two features distinguish it from earlier reviews. First, previous overviews either take in more media, dealing with audio and video as well as images [C58], or address a narrower subject, GAN-generated faces in particular [C59], or date entirely from the period before diffusion-based generation [C60]. Second, this review records cross-generator evaluation and stated limitations for each study instead of leaving them as discussion points: for every study covered, the master comparison table in Chapter 12 notes whether its detector was evaluated on generators not seen in training and which limitation, if any, its authors acknowledge.

The review makes five contributions, each developed in a later chapter:

1. A taxonomy that groups detection approaches into five families: handcrafted, statistical and frequency-domain forensics; CNN-based approaches and transfer learning; transformer and attention-based approaches; multimodal and foundation-model-based approaches; and explainability in detection (Chapter 9).
2. A comparative overview of the datasets and benchmarks used in the field for training and testing, including ArtiFact, CIFAKE and GenImage [C51, C52, C53] (Chapter 10).
3. An examination of reporting practice, showing that studies mix accuracy, AUC and average precision in varying combinations [C01, C11, C52], a variation that limits how directly they can be compared (Chapter 11).
4. The master comparison table, whose dedicated *Cross-Generator Tested?* and *Key Limitation* columns let each headline figure be read together with the conditions under which it was obtained (Chapter 12).
5. A gap analysis (Section 13.6) motivating the second, planned phase of the project, outlined in Section 14.3: a transfer-learning comparison on CIFAKE [C51] between four ImageNet-pretrained CNN backbones and a Vision Transformer benchmark [C70], with Grad-CAM [C61] to explain predictions and a subset of GenImage [C52] to test generalisation across generators.

This review itself conducts no experiments and reports none of its own; all performance figures in the chapters that follow are those reported by the cited authors.
