## 9.3 Transformer and Attention-Based Approaches

In a vision transformer (ViT), an image is handled as a sequence. It is divided into patches of a fixed size, each patch is turned into a vector by a linear embedding, and self-attention lets every patch draw on all the others from the first layer onwards [C70]. A convolutional network works the other way round, widening its view of the image only gradually as layers of local filters are stacked. For detection, the contrast has two sides. Most of the evidence described in Sections 9.1 and 9.2 is local and high-frequency. Several of the better convolutional detectors work by preserving that fine detail, one by letting each decision see only a small patch of the image [C02] and others by dropping the downsampling in the first layer, which would otherwise remove that detail [C03, C11]. A transformer's patch embedding is a stride-16 projection, so the model starts by discarding part of the information those detectors are careful to retain. In exchange, its representation is built from relations spanning the entire image, not from filter responses within a local window.

Only one surveyed study isolates the architecture. {C44} compared a ViT-Base with an EfficientNetV2-M, training both on subsets of ForgeryNet and testing them on all fifteen of its manipulation methods. The CNN turned out to be the more specialised of the two. In one experiment the training data held three face-transfer methods: FaceShifter, FS-GAN and Deepfakes. The CNN reached 81.1% on Deepfakes but only 47.0% on FaceShifter and 57.0% on FS-GAN, although all three were in its training set, while the ViT stayed close to 62.0% on each. Taking their experiments together, the authors conclude that the CNN often scores higher on the methods it was trained on but generalises worse to new ones, and that the ViT's accuracy varies less from one method to the next. They also admit that accuracy on new methods remains low for both models.

The family's second primary study adapts an existing transformer instead of training one for detection. FatFormer retains a CLIP ViT and adds a forgery-aware adapter that operates jointly on image and frequency representations, which the text side of the model helps to align [C40]. With the standard four-class ProGAN set for training, it reaches 98% accuracy on GANs and 95% on diffusion models, in both cases on generators that were absent from its training data. Because FatFormer's representation is drawn from a frozen vision-language model, Section 9.4 discusses it again. The point for this section is that the useful transformers in this literature are almost never trained from random initialisation.

Studies covered elsewhere in this review bear this out. DRCT, discussed in Section 9.2 for its contrastive training, has both ConvNeXt-B and CLIP-ViT-L/14 backbones [C32]. The Community Forensics detector, likewise discussed in Section 9.2, classifies with a CLIP-pretrained ViT-S and offers ConvNeXt-S as an alternative [C35]. The prototype method of Section 9.5 feeds the features of a fine-tuned ViT to an SVM [C64]. One secondary study also trains contrastively, with global and local similarities, on a large diffusion-image collection that it releases [C49].

None of these comparisons isolates the backbone, however. In this literature a change of backbone usually brings different pretraining data, a different training objective and a different amount of supervision, all at once. The studies therefore demonstrate that transformer-based systems did well; they do not demonstrate that the transformer is responsible. Only {C44} hold everything else constant. This is one reason why, in the second phase of this project, the ViT is planned as a benchmark for comparison with CNN backbones trained under matched conditions, not as a proposed improvement.

## 9.4 Multimodal and Foundation-Model-Based Approaches

Strictly speaking, CLIP was never built to detect anything. It pairs an image encoder with a text encoder, trained together on captioned images so that each image lands near its own caption, and the visual representation this produces carries over to tasks it was not trained on [C71]. The detectors in this section rest on an empirical finding: real and generated images are separable in that representation, although forensics played no part in its training.

{C37} first established this. They kept the weights of CLIP's ViT-L/14 image encoder fixed and only read off its features, which were classified either by a nearest-neighbour rule or by a linear probe fitted to the widely used ProGAN training set. Compared with detectors that learn their own backbone from the same data, the frozen approach improved mAP by 15.07 and accuracy by 25.90% on diffusion and autoregressive models not seen in training. Why the frozen features behave this way is, the authors admit, still an open question, and later work accepts the representation and looks for the best way to read it.

Several strategies have been tried. The simplest fits a small classifier to the frozen features: {C38} fit a linear SVM to next-to-last-layer CLIP features using a reference set that can be as small as ten paired images from a single generator, and obtain an average AUC of about 90.0% over eighteen generators, with AUC gains of 6% on out-of-distribution data and 13% on impaired images. Another reads more of the network: RINE draws on the intermediate encoder blocks, not only the final one, and learns how much weight each deserves; with four-class training it records an average accuracy of 91.5% and an AP of 98.8 across twenty test sets [C39].

A third attaches trainable components to the frozen model. Examples are FatFormer's adapter, whose 98% and 95% accuracies on unseen GANs and diffusion models were given in Section 9.3 [C40], and prompt tuning, which {C43} compare with adapters, linear probing and full fine-tuning; with it they obtain an mAP of 98.06% and an average accuracy of 89.45% over eighteen unseen generators. C2P-CLIP turns to the text side instead, where a category-common prompt injected into its text encoder carries detection semantics into the visual features. On the nineteen subsets of UniversalFakeDetect it reaches an accuracy of 93.79% with an mAP of 98.66%, and on GenImage its accuracy is 95.8% [C41]. Effort takes a geometric view, running a singular value decomposition (SVD) on the frozen features and operating within orthogonal subspaces derived from it, for an mAP of 99.41% and a mean accuracy of 95.19% on those nineteen subsets [C48].

In other work the text prompt is an input rather than part of the mechanism. DE-FAKE, aimed at text-to-image generation, sets an image-only ResNet18 against a hybrid classifier over both CLIP encoders. After training on Stable Diffusion, the hybrid achieves accuracies of 0.932 on Latent Diffusion, 0.899 on GLIDE and 0.885 on DALL·E 2, while the authors judge image-only detection on these models to fall well short of what they designed it to achieve [C26]. Further secondary studies extend these ideas to prompt-tuned vision-language models that serve directly as detectors [C42], language-guided contrastive training [C50], mixtures of low-rank experts [C47], diffusion-image detection with such features [C45] and a captioning reformulation of the task [C46].

This line of work has two gaps. The first is interpretability: the paper that started the approach and the few-shot method above both note that what CLIP features capture in forensic terms is not understood [C37, C38], a problem Section 9.5 takes up. The other gap is robustness. Under a combination of blur, cropping, compression and noise, RINE degrades considerably [C39], and with prompt tuning, accuracy on images from commercial tools drops once they are JPEG-compressed [C43]. The evaluation protocol barely changes from study to study either. Several of the detectors discussed here are fitted to ProGAN images but reported on diffusion test sets [C37, C39, C40, C43, C48]. Their headline generalisation figures therefore capture one particular shift between training and test data rather than the general case.

## 9.5 Explainability in Detection

A probability on its own gives no reason for a detector's decision. For the forensic uses described in Chapter 1 this is a genuine limitation, so a few studies ask where in the image a decision comes from. Grad-CAM, the standard tool, takes the feature maps of one convolutional layer, weights each map by how strongly the class score responds to it (its gradient), and sums the weighted maps into a coarse heatmap over the image [C61]. A later variant, Grad-CAM++, improves the weighting for sharper localisation, especially when an image contains several instances of the same class [C62]. Both methods come from object recognition, where the highlighted area contains something visible; forensic evidence, as Chapter 7 showed, often is not.

The mismatch is easy to see in the CIFAKE study. {C51} built a 120,000-image dataset of their own: 60,000 real photographs from CIFAR-10 and 60,000 images produced by Stable Diffusion v1.4. On it they trained a small custom CNN, chosen from 36 candidate topologies, with two 32-filter convolutional layers and a 64-unit dense layer; it reached 92.98% accuracy. The authors report that, according to Grad-CAM, the network's classifications rest on small background imperfections rather than on the subject. The explanation is valuable exactly because it does not flatter the detector: what the network picked up belongs to a single generator at a 32-pixel resolution, not to synthesis in general.

{C02} approach localisation from the opposite side. Their patch classifiers, covered in Section 9.2, confine each decision to a small region by limiting the receptive field, and combining the per-patch scores yields a map of the image regions that are detectable at all. Such a map explains the evidence, not a particular model's reasoning, and one cannot stand in for the other.

{C64} prefer interpretability by construction to explanation after the fact, classifying the features of a fine-tuned ViT with an SVM whose support vectors serve as prototypes to which any decision can be traced back. Accuracy within one dataset is high: with the fine-tuned ViT followed by an MLP head they report 99.7% on a class-conditional ImageNet set, 96.7% on an image-to-image ImageNet set, 99.5% on Oxford-IIIT Pet and 99.4% on FFHQ, and the SVM on the same features reaches 93.0–97.2%. Their cross-dataset tests give lower figures, 81.3% and 84.0%. These tests say nothing about unseen generators: the fakes in both the training and the test data came from Stable Diffusion, so the image content changed but the generator did not. The authors also mention having to build the datasets themselves, as no open ones were available.

Only recently have such explanations been evaluated quantitatively. {C63} put forward an evaluation framework and test it on an EfficientNetV2 detector with FaceForensics++. Grad-CAM++, RISE, SHAP, LIME and SOBOL are compared on one common measure: how far the detector's accuracy falls once the image segments that each method ranks highest have been altered; the lower the accuracy that remains, the better the explanation. LIME comes out on top: altering only its highest-ranked segment left the detector with an accuracy of 0.579 on NeuralTextures, and altering its three highest-ranked segments brought accuracy on DeepFakes down to 0.245. The type of forgery also matters: the authors find Face2Face and FaceSwap fakes the hardest to explain. The base is narrow, one detector on one dataset, but the study shows that explanation quality can be measured rather than left to inspection.

The open problem is the step from showing where a network looked to stating what it found there. A heatmap can reveal a shortcut, as with CIFAKE, yet it leaves open which property of the underlying pixels is the synthetic one. The frozen-feature detectors of Section 9.4 are further still from an answer, since their authors acknowledge not knowing what the representation encodes [C37, C38]. CIFAKE also links this chapter to the next. Its contribution combines a dataset with an explainability analysis [C51], and Chapter 10 turns to the datasets and benchmarks on which the field relies.
